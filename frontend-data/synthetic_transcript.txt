INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPT — SENIOR QA AUTOMATION ENGINEER
Candidate: Natalia Voss
Interviewer: Marek Jabłoński, Engineering Manager – Platform Quality
Date: Synthetic / For RAG Evaluation Purposes Only
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Interviewer: Natalia, thanks for making the time today. I've had a chance to read through
your CV and I have to say, the Lendio Solutions migration project jumped out at me
immediately. But before we get into the details, can you give me a quick thirty-second
pitch — who are you professionally, and what gets you excited about quality work?

Candidate: Sure, absolutely. So, in short — I'm someone who came up through manual
testing and taught herself automation because she got tired of doing the same regression
cycle by hand every two weeks. That background matters to me because I think it gives
me a different lens than engineers who started directly in code. I still think like a tester
first, even when I'm writing a framework. What genuinely excites me is that moment where
a pipeline catches a regression before a developer even opens a pull request. That
feedback loop — getting quality upstream rather than downstream — that's the thing I keep
optimising for.

Interviewer: I like that framing. Let's dig into the Playwright migration you led at Lendio.
The numbers you've quoted in your CV are striking — forty-seven minutes down to eleven,
and three hundred and forty flaky tests eliminated. Can you walk me through that project
using the STAR structure? I want to understand the full arc of it.

Candidate: Yeah, of course. So — the Situation. When I joined Lendio in March 2021, the
team had a Selenium suite that had been accumulating technical debt for about four years.
It was running in Java, using a page-object model that nobody had really maintained
consistently. The suite took around forty-seven minutes to complete on our CI runner, and
on any given Monday morning we'd have maybe thirty to fifty tests marked as flaky just
from the weekend nightly runs. The flakiness was coming from a mix of things — hardcoded
sleeps, tests sharing state through a single database, and a few tests that were genuinely
timing-sensitive because they relied on third-party sandbox APIs.

Interviewer: And what was the business impact of that before you intervened?

Candidate: Good question. The direct impact was that engineers had basically learned to
ignore red builds unless the failure count crossed some informal threshold they'd set in
their heads. So the suite had lost credibility. PRs were being merged with failing tests
because people assumed it was "just flakiness." The broader impact was that any genuine
regression was getting lost in the noise.

Interviewer: Right, the classic wolf-crying problem. So what was your specific Task in
that context?

Candidate: My task — and this was something I proposed rather than was assigned,
which I think is worth noting — was to rebuild the suite in a way that restored trust.
The explicit goal I set with my manager was: get the build to green consistently, with
fewer than two flaky failures per week on average, and get the full regression runtime
under fifteen minutes so it could plausibly run on every PR rather than just nightly.

Interviewer: That's a well-defined target. How did you actually approach the Actions?
Because migrating a suite that size is not a small undertaking.

Candidate: No, it isn't, and I had to do it while the team was still shipping features —
so a big-bang rewrite wasn't an option. The first thing I did was a flakiness audit. I
pulled three months of CI logs and categorised every failure by root cause. About forty
percent of the flakiness came from that shared-database-state problem, another thirty
percent from the third-party sandbox API timeouts, and the remaining thirty percent
was a mix of CSS selector brittleness and those hardcoded sleeps.

Interviewer: Mm-hmm.

Candidate: So rather than just "switching to Playwright," which is what some people
might jump to, I tackled the database isolation problem first — because that would give
me wins in the existing suite while I built the new one in parallel. I wrote the test-data
provisioning service I mention in my CV, which used SQLAlchemy and Faker to spin up
isolated schema state per test run. That alone eliminated about a hundred and forty of
the flaky tests within six weeks, which gave the team some immediate relief and, honestly,
gave me some political capital to pursue the bigger migration.

Then, over the following three months, I rebuilt the suite incrementally in Playwright
with TypeScript. The selector strategy shifted to accessibility-role-based locators — which
are inherently more stable than CSS selectors — and I replaced the third-party API
timeouts with a local mock server using WireMock-style stubs. I also pushed for parallelisation
at the worker level, which Playwright handles much more gracefully than Selenium Grid
did for us.

Interviewer: Were there any points where the migration almost went sideways?

Candidate: Umm — yeah, actually. About halfway through, we had a sprint where three
feature teams were all landing significant changes simultaneously, and my parallel-build
strategy meant I was maintaining two suites at once. There was a two-week period where
I was genuinely stretched thin, and a couple of new features slipped through without
full coverage in the new suite. I had to have a direct conversation with my manager and
the squad leads about temporarily pausing new feature automation to let me catch up.
That was uncomfortable, but the alternative — shipping half-finished automation — would
have created exactly the technical debt I was trying to pay off. So, yeah, I've learned that
migration projects need an explicit "slow down to speed up" conversation early, not when
you're already in it.

Interviewer: That's a mature reflection. And the Results?

Candidate: The Results — by January 2022, about nine months after I started, the
full suite was on Playwright, running in eleven minutes on average across three browser
targets. Flaky failures dropped to under one per week in the first quarter, and over the
following six months we had zero weeks where failures exceeded two. More importantly —
and this is the metric I care about most — the team's behaviour changed. Developers
started actually looking at CI results before merging. The suite had its credibility back.
We also noticed a secondary benefit: onboarding new engineers became faster because
TypeScript with Playwright's auto-waiting API was significantly easier for them to reason
about than the old Java-plus-explicit-wait pattern.

Interviewer: Excellent. Let me shift gears slightly. You mention contract testing with Pact
as well. For candidates who claim contract testing experience, it can sometimes be surface-level.
Can you describe a concrete scenario where it caught something meaningful?

Candidate: Sure. We had a situation — umm, this was late 2022 — where a backend team
was refactoring a payments microservice. They changed the structure of an error response
object: moved a field called `decline_reason` from the top level of the JSON into a nested
`metadata` object. It was a well-intentioned refactor from their perspective — better
schema hygiene. But two consumer services were reading `decline_reason` from the top
level. Under the old regime — pre-Pact — this kind of change would have been caught
either in staging integration testing or, worst case, in production. With the consumer-driven
contracts in place, the CI pipeline on the payments service failed as soon as the schema
change was committed, because the consumer contracts published by the two dependent
services explicitly stated that `decline_reason` should exist at the root level. The breaking
change was caught within minutes of the commit. The payments team updated their contract
negotiation with the consumers, both parties agreed on a migration path, and it was handled
through a versioned API deprecation cycle rather than an emergency hotfix.

Interviewer: That's exactly the kind of story I was hoping to hear. Last question — where
do you see your growth edge right now? What's the area of quality engineering you'd
most like to develop in your next role?

Candidate: Honestly, it's the observability side. I've worked with Datadog and the ELK
stack in a "read the dashboards" capacity, and I've wired up synthetic monitors, but I
haven't had the opportunity to design a full observability strategy from scratch — deciding
what signals matter, building the alerting logic, connecting that feedback loop back into
the test suite. I'm also genuinely curious about applying quality thinking to AI/ML outputs —
validating RAG pipelines, evaluating embedding retrieval accuracy, that kind of thing. I've
been doing some self-directed exploration there, but it's still early for me. I'd want to be
honest about that.

Interviewer: I appreciate the candour. Natalia, this has been a really substantive
conversation. We'll be in touch within the week about next steps. Thanks for your time.

Candidate: Thank you, Marek. I've enjoyed the conversation — it was a good one.
